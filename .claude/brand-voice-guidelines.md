## Voice Attributes

### 1. Conversational Authority (Primary)
**Confidence:** High
Write like you're telling someone the story at a bar, not writing a LinkedIn post. Use short, declarative sentences. Break grammar rules when it sounds more natural ("That's the job." "Simple." "We didn't do that."). You've directed campaigns for the biggest brands on earth — that authority is embedded in the stories, not announced upfront.

**Examples:**
- "I was Creative Director on Orangetheory Fitness for five years. Scaled the brand from 250 studios to 1,300 worldwide."
- "Get that wrong and you don't just miss — you annoy. You become that person at the party who won't stop talking about themselves."
- "Good ideas don't care about time zones."

### 2. Story-First Structure (Primary)
**Confidence:** High
Every piece of content starts with a story, observation, or provocation — never with credentials. The hook is the tension, the insight, or the unexpected angle. Credentials and self-promotion come at the end, if at all. The reader should get value even if they never reach the CTA.

**Pattern:** Hook (tension/story) → Context (what happened) → Insight (why it matters) → Takeaway (the lesson) → Credentials/CTA (buried at the end, 1-2 lines max)

**Examples:**
- Opens with "The brief was simple. Too simple." — not "I'm an award-winning CD who worked on Orangetheory."
- Opens with "OpenAI is testing ads inside ChatGPT." — not "As a Creative Director with 15 years of experience, here's my take on AI ads."

### 3. Opinionated but Generous (Primary)
**Confidence:** High
Take a clear stance. Don't hedge with "it depends" or "there are many perspectives." Say what you actually think. But frame opinions as lessons learned from experience, not hot takes for engagement. Share the thinking behind the opinion so others can learn from it.

**Examples:**
- "Advertising likes to pretend ideas come from brainstorm sessions and strategy decks. Some of them do. But the best ones — the ones with texture, the ones that feel alive — those come from actually looking at the world."
- "AI is the most powerful creative tool we've ever been given. I use it every day. It makes me faster. But a tool without direction is just noise."

### 4. Finding the Real Question (Primary)
**Confidence:** High
The brief clients hand you is rarely the real problem. Noah's signature move is looking past the stated ask to find the tension underneath — what's actually standing in the way. This isn't just a creative technique; it's a worldview that shapes how every piece of content is framed. When telling a story, show the discovery process: what the brief said, what you found when you dug deeper, and why that shift mattered.

**Examples:**
- "The brief was 'drive memberships.' The real brief was 'people don't have time.'"
- "It can't read a client's face and realise the real problem isn't what they wrote down."
- "Don't ask 'how do we sell this?' Ask 'what's actually standing in the way?'"

### 5. Cultural References as Credibility Anchors (Secondary)
**Confidence:** Medium (sparse examples — use sparingly)
Occasionally cite credible creative peers (designers, directors, cultural figures) to validate a point — never to name-drop or posture. The quote should do intellectual work, not decoration. Use 1-2x per month maximum. Reference people Noah's audience would respect: creative leaders, not business influencers.

**Example:**
- "As Virgil Abloh once said, 'Creative Director is a catch-all phrase for giving ideas. To me, it obviously means more than that.' He was right. It always has."

### 6. Specific and Grounded (Secondary)
**Confidence:** High
Use real numbers, real brand names, and real details. Never say "a major brand" when you can say "Coca-Cola." Never say "great results" when you can say "$4 million in revenue in a single day." Specificity is what separates credible from generic.

**Examples:**
- "3.75 billion impressions and 98% positive sentiment" — not "massive reach and positive response"
- "An iced Americano that costs about a dollar fifty" — not "affordable coffee"
- "250 to 1,300+ studios worldwide" — not "significant growth"

## Sentence-Level Style

**Rhythm:** Mix short punchy sentences with longer narrative ones. Use sentence fragments intentionally for emphasis. One-line paragraphs are a signature move.

- "We didn't do that."
- "That's the job. Not making ads. Finding the tension in someone's life and building everything around it."
- "Simple."

**Punctuation:** Use em dashes liberally for asides and emphasis. Avoid semicolons (too formal). Occasional rhetorical questions are fine but don't overdo them.

**Vocabulary:** Plain language. No jargon unless it's industry-specific and your audience would use it naturally ("integrated 360," "tentpole activation," "earned impressions"). Never use corporate filler: "leverage," "synergy," "best-in-class," "results-driven," "passionate."

**Contractions:** Always. "I'm," "didn't," "won't," "that's." Never "I am" or "did not" unless for dramatic emphasis.

**Person:** First person singular ("I") for personal content. Occasionally shift to second person ("you") to pull the reader in. Never third person about yourself.

**British-ish spelling:** Noah naturally uses British spellings from living abroad — "romanticise," "colour," "realise." These should appear organically (1-2 per longer piece), not in every sentence. Don't force American spellings back in, but don't overdo the British either. It's an authentic texture, not an affectation.

## Platform-Specific Rules

### LinkedIn
- Long-form, story-driven. This is the primary platform for depth.
- No hashtags in the body of the post.
- Self-promo goes in the last two lines, maximum.
- Every post should sound like you talking, not you "creating content."
- One availability post per month, max. And make it earn its place — wrap it in something useful.

### Instagram
- Visual-first. Carousels for case studies (punchy text slides). Photo dumps for Bangkok life.
- Captions are conversational, shorter than LinkedIn, but still have a point of view.
- No hashtags in the caption. Put 3-5 in the first comment if needed for discoverability.
- Memes use your brand design system (colors, type), never stock meme templates.

### TikTok
- Hook in the first 3 seconds or you're dead.
- Talking head + quick cuts. Under 90 seconds.
- Same voice but faster — bar-story speed, not essay speed.
- Use trending sounds sparingly and only when they actually fit.
- Hashtags in caption only.

### Cross-Platform
- Never post the same thing on all three platforms. Adapt the idea, don't copy-paste.
- LinkedIn gets the full story. Instagram gets the visual version. TikTok gets the fast version.
- Credentials go at the end or not at all. Lead with the story or the opinion.

## Voice Reference Model

The voice is internally described as the **"Rodd Chant model"** — named after the creative director and LinkedIn creator known for conversational, opinionated, story-driven content with zero corporate polish. The comparison means: write like a senior creative who has seen everything, respects the craft deeply, and talks about it the way you'd talk to a colleague over drinks — not the way you'd write a press release.

## What to Avoid

- Corporate LinkedIn voice ("I'm thrilled to announce..." / "Here are 5 tips for...")
- Hashtags in LinkedIn post bodies
- Self-promotion in the first half of any post
- Stock meme templates (always use brand design system)
- Romanticizing the nomad lifestyle
- Vague claims without numbers or specifics
- Hedging on opinions ("I think maybe possibly...")
- Motivational poster energy ("Hustle harder!" / "Rise and grind!")
- Content that sounds like a brand social team wrote it
- More than one availability post per month
- Copy-pasting the same content across platforms

**Green divider line:** 60px wide, 3px tall — used as a section break between headline and body copy

**Tag / pill system:** Outlined rectangles (no fill) with #C7F042 stroke at 25% opacity. Green text inside. Used for categorization (brand name, discipline, format). Height: 40px, horizontal padding ~20px.

### Meme Visual Rules

- Two-panel split (top/bottom), divided by green accent line
- Same typography system as all other templates — never a stock meme generator
- Top panel: the setup (clean, simple statement)
- Bottom panel: the punchline (more text, slightly smaller font to fit)
- Warm gradient on bottom panel for subtle depth
- Available in 4x5, 1x1, and 9x16

### Grain Variant

Banner templates have a "-Grain" variant available. Use for a more textured, editorial feel when appropriate. Same layout, same colors, with a film grain overlay.

### Website-Specific Design

**Spacing:** Section padding 120px top/bottom, card gaps 24px, inner card padding 32-40px, nav height ~64px
**Interactions:** Cards lift 4px on hover with green border, fade-up animations on scroll (IntersectionObserver), smooth scrolling between sections, green selection highlight
**Images:** Gradient overlay on project cards ensures text readability over any background image

### Visual Voice Principles

1. **Dark-first.** The default is always dark mode. Light mode exists for variety and contrast in carousels, not as the primary expression.
2. **Typography does the heavy lifting.** The design system is type-driven, not image-driven. Bold headlines, tight tracking, generous whitespace. The words are the visual.
3. **Green is the signature.** #C7F042 is the most distinctive element — it marks what matters. Use it for emphasis, never for decoration.
4. **Restraint over flash.** No gradients on text, no drop shadows, no glow effects. The system is flat, minimal, and confident. Let the content breathe.
5. **Everything looks like the same person made it.** Whether it's a meme, a case study carousel, a LinkedIn banner, or a YouTube thumbnail — the visual language is identical. Same fonts, same colors, same spacing, same attitude.
